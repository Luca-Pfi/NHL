Contribution

The top six score; the bottom six do the heavy lifting

Each forward's role comes from his average ice time for that team that season. In every game the six dressed forwards with the most role ice time are the Top 6 and the rest are the Bottom 6.

  • The top six play 59% of forward minutes and score 70% of forward goals, 85% of power-play goals and 72% of game-winners.
  • At 5-on-5 the gap is much smaller. The bottom six score 36% of forward 5-on-5 goals on 42% of the even-strength time. Most of the top six's scoring edge comes from the power play.
  • The bottom six deliver 59% of forward hits and 47% of forward blocked shots, and take 48% of the penalties.

Minute for minute, a bottom-six forward produces 61% of a top-six forward's goals, 73% of the expected goals and 82% of the 5-on-5 goals. He hits 2.1 times as often and takes 33% more penalties. Bottom-six forwards shoot 10.2% against 13.3% for the top six, and they finish 0.08 goals per 60 below their expected goals.

At even strength the top six outscore opponents 2.89 to 2.69 per 60 minutes, while the bottom six are outscored 2.10 to 2.37. The bottom six actually allow fewer goals per 60. Their problem is offense. Only 34% of bottom-six groups in the sample had a positive even-strength goal differential, against 64% of top-six groups.

Bottom-six goals are not cheap. Each adds 15.1% win probability on average, against 16.2% for a top-six goal. Half of them tie the game or take the lead, the same rate as top-six goals. One more goal multiplies the odds of winning by 3.29 from the bottom six and 3.35 from the top six, which is statistically the same.

Winning

A depth goal matters most when the stars go quiet

Teams win 62% of games in which the bottom six score and 36% of games in which they don't. The top six score one goal or fewer in 45% of games, and those are the nights depth decides.

Across a season

A team-season model of points % (160 team-seasons, R² = 0.93) splits goal differential into four parts. A goal of differential is worth about the same whichever group produces it. The groups differ in how much teams vary on each part. Top-six even-strength goal differential explains 35% of the explained gap between teams, the bottom six 25%, special teams 23%, and empty-net and extra-attacker goals 17%. Among forwards that is a 59/41 split, almost exactly their 58/42 split of even-strength ice time.

Theory · no bottom six

What would the top six need if the bottom six disappeared?

Three ways to look at it, from most literal to most hypothetical.

Lens 1 · erase depth scoring

Remove every bottom-six goal and re-score the games

  • Win % falls from 50.0% to 39.6% and points % from .556 to .447. That is 8.5 wins and 17.8 standings points per 82 games, enough to turn a 91-point bubble team into a 73-point lottery team.
  • 30% of all wins needed at least one bottom-six goal.
  • To make up for it the top six would need 2.62 goals a game instead of 1.86, a 41% increase. Only 1 of 160 team-seasons had a top six that productive. The best managed 2.74.

Lens 2 · a replacement-level bottom six

How good must the top six be to still make the playoffs?

Instead of zero goals, give the team the worst bottom six in the sample. The playoff line sits at a points % of about .564.

Lens 3 · the two-line team

Only six forwards dress and the stars take every shift

The top six would go from about 18 to about 30 minutes a night, with roughly half the rest between shifts. The model uses each group's measured even-strength goals for and against per 60, and applies a fatigue penalty f to the top six: goals for drop by f and goals against rise by f. The break-even fatigue is the f at which two lines are exactly as good as the real twelve-forward lineup.

On the second night of a back-to-back, top-six expected goals drop 5.1% (±0.8%), top-six points drop 7.2%, team goals for drop 6.7% and goals against rise 6.0%. One day of accumulated fatigue already costs more than the 3.5% an average team can afford. A two-line team would add twelve extra minutes every night inside the same game.

A team with no bottom six works only on paper. It needs an elite top six and a bottom six so bad that thirty-minute stars are still the better option.

Theory · the right bottom six

Which bottom-six traits help any team win?

Each trait below is correlated with points % after controlling for top-six quality, special teams and goaltending (goals saved above expected). Orange points have a 90% interval that excludes zero.

The bottom-six checklist

  • +Don't lose your minutes. Even-strength goals against per 60 is the biggest single lever (partial r = −0.46). A bottom six that breaks even beats one that scores more but gives up chances.
  • +Chip in about a goal a night. The average bottom six scores 0.78 a game, and the lift is largest when the stars go cold.
  • +Finish what you get. Bottom-six groups that beat their expected goals win more (partial r = +0.20).
  • +Stay out of the box and draw penalties. The bottom six take 33% more penalties per minute than the top six, and penalty differential is a significant positive trait (+0.15).
  • −Don't pay for hits, blocks or faceoffs. They are the most visible bottom-six stats and show no link to winning once goal differential is known (hits −0.05, blocks +0.03, faceoff win % −0.09).

Moving a bottom six from the 10th to the 90th percentile is worth about 10.5 standings points per 82 games. The same move for a top six is worth 18.7. A depth upgrade is worth a little more than half a top-six upgrade in the standings. Whether it is the better buy depends on price, and salaries are not part of this dataset.

Playoffs

Every recent champion had a bottom six that won its minutes

  • In the playoffs the bottom six play slightly less (39.1% of forward ice time against 40.6%) and score slightly less (22.8% of team goals against 25.2%).
  • Across 75 series, the winner had the better bottom-six even-strength goal differential 71% of the time, a little more often than it had the better top-six goal differential (67%).
  • All five champions had a bottom six with a positive even-strength goal differential. The other playoff teams' bottom-six groups averaged negative every year.

Method and limits

How the numbers were built

Data

Boxscores and full play-by-play for every regular-season and playoff game from 2021-22 to 2025-26: 6,560 regular-season games, 433 playoff games and 160 team-seasons, from the public NHL web API (api-web.nhle.com).

Roles

Top 6 means the six dressed forwards with the highest season-average ice time for that team. Defining the roles by each game's ice time instead gives the same results: the bottom six score 25.9% of team goals instead of 25.2%, and teams win 63.7% (vs 62.4%) when they score.

Models

An expected-goals model (shot distance, angle, type, rebound, rush, manpower; AUC 0.77, trained leave-one-season-out) and an in-game win-probability model (clock and score; AUC 0.81). Every goal is valued by the win probability it added.

Even-strength on-ice goals

The public API has no shift data, so each group's on-ice goals for and against are rebuilt from plus/minus, goal and assist credits, and each team's even-strength, power-play and penalty-kill seconds. The groups' totals match the real totals (2.142 vs 2.146 goals against per game).

Limits

On-ice figures are estimates, not shift-level truth, and lines are not perfectly separated. Quality of competition is not controlled, so bottom-six goals-against rates are flattered. Team-level results rest on 160 team-seasons and show correlation, not cause. The two-line team is a theoretical model whose fatigue penalty is a free parameter.

Reproduce

All code, tables and charts are in github.com/Luca-Pfi/NHL. One script fetches the games, builds the tables, fits the models and redraws every chart.

Regular season unless marked as playoffs. Wins include overtime and shootout wins; shootout goals are excluded from all scoring stats.
